# Chapter 7: Dante II: Purgatory and Paradise

*Mainly draws on Great Books #10–12 (Beijing high-school class, April–May 2026), the Dante seminar sessions #1–5 (Paradise) and #9–12 (Purgatory), livestreamed June 2026, and the earlier Civilization #29–30 (January 2025).*

## The story he tells

In Jiang's telling, the *Divine Comedy* is a contest between two poets. Homer taught that love binds the universe and that each person can connect to "the source". Virgil, a servant of empire, cut that connection and preached piety and obedience, which led to a thousand years of stagnation. Dante ends that era: "Dante must defeat Virgil in order to restore our humanity" [GB 12 · 04:09](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=249); [GB 12 · 01:06](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=66). (For Dante's life, Beatrice and Virgil as guide, see Chapter 6.)

Purgatory is where the contest turns. One figure after another breaks Virgil's rule that only baptized Christians can be saved. Cato, a pagan who killed himself, guards the mountain. Statius, Virgil's own devotee, climbs past him. The Trojan Ripheus sits in heaven. So "clearly Virgil's framework is wrong" [GB 11 · 58:54](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=3534). The poets Dante meets on the way (Casella, Sordello, Forese, Guinizelli) show what purgation really is. Souls are held back by their own failure to forgive themselves, not by God [GB 11 · 34:43](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2083), and it's the prayers of the people who love them that speed them up, not bribes to the Church [Dante 10 · 2:03:14](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=7394). At the summit Virgil vanishes and an angry Beatrice arrives. Jiang calls her anger "just indignation", the same feeling that "propelled his creativity" and made Dante write the poem [Dante 12 · 3:53:53](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=14033).

Paradise is not a place to rest. It is higher education: "heaven is fundamentally about debate, dialogue and philosophy" [GB 12 · 06:06](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=366). Behind an orthodox front, its residents carry Dante's hidden program. Piccarda shows what fear does to the will. Justinian recites the Church's official history, which Dante then undercuts. Aquinas and Bonaventure explain why reform movements go bad. Cacciaguida turns the imperial prophecy of the *Aeneid* into a prophecy of exile. The apostles examine Dante, and he answers, in effect, "God is love." At the top, Bernard and Mary hand him on to a God who, in Jiang's reading, needs a human imagination in order to know itself [GB 12 · 22:35](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1355). He presents all of this as a hidden revolution, one that would have gotten Dante burned if "the Catholic Church actually understood what Dante was doing" [Dante 4 · 53:27](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=3207).

## The main figures

### Cato the Younger

*Who:* Marcus Porcius Cato (95–46 BC), a Stoic Roman senator who opposed Julius Caesar and killed himself at Utica rather than accept Caesar's pardon. Dante makes him the guardian of Mount Purgatory (Purgatory 1–2). His Roman life is in Chapter 4.

**What he teaches**
- Cato is a triple paradox. He was a pagan born before Christ. He rebelled against Caesar, whose killers Brutus and Cassius are in Lucifer's jaws. And he committed suicide, which the Church of the time taught sent you straight to hell. Yet he is "master and guardian of purgatory" [GB 10 · 35:50](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2150); [Dante 9 · 2:26:48](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=8808). The paradox "forces us to think about what's really going on here" [GB 10 · 36:44](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2204).
- He does not romanticize the suicide. Cato died to deny Caesar the chance to show him mercy, which Jiang calls "kind of petty really" [Dante 9 · 2:27:45](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=8865).
- For Jiang, the meeting with Virgil exposes Virgil. The two were contemporaries in Rome and in Limbo, so you'd expect a happy reunion. Instead Virgil is deferential, almost afraid, and brings up Cato's wife Marcia, still in Limbo, in a way that is "almost like a bribe" [GB 10 · 39:20](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2360); [GB 10 · 40:47](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2447). "So, they're like enemies" [Dante 9 · 2:35:39](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=9339). Picking up a student's idea, he reads Cato's order to wash Dante's face as Dante beginning "to cleanse himself of Virgil's lies" [Dante 9 · 2:36:42](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=9402).
- His reading of Cato's coldness toward Marcia has changed over time. In January 2025, Cato decides she must be less virtuous than he thought, because she did not rise with him [Civ 30 · 24:29](https://youtu.be/asbKjezwIJM?t=1469). By April 2026 it had become a point about free will: Cato chose to reflect on himself and climb, he can't make Marcia do the same, and forcing her would not be love [GB 10 · 44:08](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2648).
- Later Cato becomes his evidence. When Virgil claims he lost heaven only because he lacked faith, Jiang says we know this is false because "we just saw Kato in purgatory" [Dante 10 · 2:29:48](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8988).

**In his big picture:** Cato is the first proof that in Dante's universe, salvation depends on will and honest self-reflection, not on baptism or obedience.

**In his words:** "It does you no good to force anyone to do anything. That's not what love is." [GB 10 · 44:46](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2686)

**Reality check:** Commentators usually explain Cato's coldness by the rule he states himself: once freed, he can no longer be moved by souls on the far side of the river. Reading it as personal enmity toward Virgil is Jiang's interpretation.

### Manfred

*Who:* Manfred (c. 1232–1266), King of Sicily, son of Emperor Frederick II and grandson of Empress Constance. The papacy excommunicated him, and he was killed fighting Charles of Anjou at Benevento. Dante finds him among the late-repentant excommunicates at the foot of the mountain (Purgatory 3).

**What he teaches**
- Excommunication was a "go straight to hell card": it cut you off from the Christian community no matter what you believed or did. Yet Manfred is saved [Dante 9 · 3:32:32](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=12752).
- The reason is that he turned to God in tears as he died. "You have a direct connection to God," and excommunication "just means you spend more time in purgatory" [Dante 9 · 3:33:12](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=12792). He calls this revolutionary for its time.
- Manfred asks that his daughter pray for him. Jiang uses this to teach the rule that prayers of the living shorten the wait, and that prayers from more people, prayed more earnestly, count for more [Dante 9 · 3:33:43](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=12823).

**In his big picture:** Manfred shows the Church's verdicts counting for less than a person's direct bond with God.

**In his words:** "What matters is your faith in God." [Dante 9 · 3:33:24](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=12804)

### Sordello

*Who:* Sordello da Goito (c. 1200–1269), a troubadour from near Mantua who wrote in Provençal. In Purgatory 6–8 he embraces Virgil as a fellow Mantuan and shows the poets the Valley of the Princes.

**What he teaches**
- Sordello is a troubadour, a poet "of the courtly love tradition". Because he and Virgil come from the same place, they embrace, and Virgil is visibly delighted [Dante 10 · 2:16:33](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8193).
- The embrace sets off Dante's attack on a divided Italy. Jiang reads it as a return to "the prophetic tradition": Italy is full of sin, "like Sodom Gomorrah". He sets it against the canto's other theme, Dante's vanity about the shadow that makes souls notice him [Dante 10 · 2:20:14](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8414); [Dante 10 · 2:20:42](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8442).
- When Sordello asks who he is, Virgil says he lost heaven only for lack of faith. Jiang calls this a lie Virgil tells himself, and explains it as cognitive dissonance: the meeting with Cato was so traumatic "that he just blocked it out" [Dante 10 · 2:31:00](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=9060); [Dante 10 · 2:31:52](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=9112).
- Sordello is the control case for Statius. Both poets revere Virgil, but Statius gets "the opposite reaction" [Dante 11 · 2:42:43](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=9763).

**In his big picture:** Sordello is one of the poets who fill Purgatory while Dante searches for his own voice (see Guinizelli below).

**In his words:** "And you can see how happy and enthusiastic Virgil is to meet someone from the same area." [Dante 10 · 2:16:44](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8204)

### Statius

*Who:* Publius Papinius Statius (c. AD 45–96), Roman author of the *Thebaid* and the unfinished *Achilleid*, who openly revered Virgil. Dante has him finish his purgation during the climb (Purgatory 21–22) and go with the poets to the summit.

**What he teaches**
- The mountain shakes whenever a soul is cleansed, and this time the soul is Statius, finished after more than 500 years [GB 11 · 30:39](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=1839). His line that the will alone proves purity means souls stay because they choose to: "The issue is that we have not forgiven ourselves" [GB 11 · 33:54](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2034); [GB 11 · 34:43](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2083).
- This corrects Virgil's theory of love. Love also means "restraint and judgement": a soul must make itself a perfect pearl before it goes to God [GB 11 · 36:04](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2164).
- The paradox is that Statius worships Virgil ("the Aeneid was my mother"), yet the disciple goes to heaven while the master stays in Limbo [GB 11 · 38:34](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2314); [GB 11 · 38:57](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2337).
- Virgil signals Dante to keep quiet and is mortified when Statius kneels to kiss his feet. Jiang's analogy: a former student comes back as a Harvard professor of Dante, and the teacher, still in the same job twenty years later, suddenly has to leave [GB 11 · 43:26](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2606); [GB 11 · 44:16](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2656).
- Statius says Virgil's verses made him a secret, baptized Christian, and that he spent four centuries on the terrace of sloth for being half-hearted about it [GB 11 · 49:54](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2994); [Dante 11 · 3:17:03](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=11823). Jiang's reading is that Statius "purposely misread Virgil": he took the poetry and trusted his own intuition over the doctrine. The episode is "subtly and with nuance undermining the argument of Virgil" [GB 11 · 52:27](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=3147). He read it the same way in January 2025 [Civ 30 · 39:57](https://youtu.be/asbKjezwIJM?t=2397).

**In his big picture:** Statius is the pupil who goes beyond the master. He is Jiang's model reader, who takes the beauty of an authority's work and rejects its worldview.

**In his words:** "He loved Virgil for his poetry, but instead of believing Virgil, he in his heart chose to believe in himself." [GB 11 · 50:42](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=3042)

**Reality check:** There is no historical evidence that Statius was a Christian. His conversion is Dante's invention, probably built on the medieval reading of Virgil's Fourth Eclogue as a prophecy of Christ.

### Forese Donati

*Who:* Forese Donati (d. 1296), a friend from Dante's youth and brother of Piccarda and of the Black Guelf leader Corso Donati. He and Dante traded insulting sonnets. Dante finds him starved thin on the terrace of gluttony (Purgatory 23–24).

**What he teaches**
- Forese is Dante's "childhood best friend" and a fellow poet [Dante 11 · 3:28:18](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12498).
- The family map: his sister is Piccarda, whom we meet in heaven. His brother Corso took Piccarda out of her convent because he was "intent on power", and will "die and burn in hell" [Dante 11 · 3:28:40](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12520).
- The puzzle is that Forese has been dead less than five years and is already high on the mountain. The answer is that his widow Nella prayed for him every day, and because she was especially virtuous he rose quickly. Jiang says this also explains the infants in paradise [Dante 11 · 3:32:19](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12739).

**In his big picture:** Forese shows the love and prayer of ordinary people moving souls toward God. Here the Church is not the channel.

**In his words:** "He's also a poet and they enjoy making fun of each other through their poetry." [Dante 11 · 3:28:18](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12498)

### Guido Guinizelli

*Who:* Guido Guinizelli (c. 1230–1276), Bolognese poet regarded as the father of the *dolce stil novo*, the "sweet new style" of love poetry that Dante took up. Dante meets him in the purifying fire of the lustful (Purgatory 26), alongside the troubadour Arnaut Daniel.

**What he teaches**
- Jiang calls him "probably the most famous Florentine poet" of the time and someone Dante "really admires". He first says the two were friends, then corrects himself: Dante did not know him but is "obviously a huge fan" [Dante 12 · 1:04:10](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=3850); [Dante 12 · 1:07:10](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=4030).
- Purgatory is full of poets (Sordello, Statius, Guinizelli) because the climb is Dante's search for "his poetic voice", for a way to stand out from the crowd, "something that haunts Dante" [Dante 12 · 1:04:30](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=3870).
- Dante pays homage to Guinizelli and Arnaut, but they are indifferent, because fame no longer matters to them [Dante 12 · 1:07:10](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=4030).
- Straight after comes the wall of fire, where for the first time Dante doubts Virgil. Virgil gets him through only by invoking Beatrice, which marks the journey "from Virgil to Beatatric" [Dante 12 · 1:10:29](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=4229); [Dante 12 · 1:11:51](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=4311).

**In his big picture:** Dante's literary heroes teach him to give up the hunger for fame that drives his vanity through Purgatory.

**In his words:** "all they're concerned about is redemption. Like I don't care about my former fame." [Dante 12 · 1:07:10](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=4030)

**Reality check:** Guinizelli was from Bologna, not Florence, and died when Dante was about eleven.

### Matelda

*Who:* The lady Dante finds gathering flowers in the Earthly Paradise at the top of the mountain (Purgatory 28–33). She explains the garden and bathes him in the rivers Lethe and Eunoe. Scholars disagree about who she represents.

**What he teaches**
- Matelda is the "guardian of the Garden of Eden", and Dante turns the Bible upside down: Eden, the place we were banished from, becomes the gate into paradise [Dante 12 · 1:42:54](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6174).
- This Eden's rivers are not the biblical ones. Lethe comes from Greek myth and Eunoe is a word Dante made up (roughly "good mind"). Lethe wipes out the memory of sin and Eunoe restores the memory of good deeds [Dante 12 · 1:44:00](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6240); [Dante 12 · 1:44:12](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6252).
- Why wash away memory? Because sin "is trauma" done to one's own soul, and its memory is the last trace of the body, so it has to be washed off before heaven [Dante 12 · 1:52:50](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6770). You choose penance, but "you cannot actually choose to forget" [Dante 12 · 1:49:10](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6550).
- He agrees with a student that what Matelda performs is "literally baptism" [Dante 12 · 1:54:16](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6856).

**In his big picture:** Matelda completes Purgatory's psychology. Healing needs both forgetting the damage and remembering the good.

**In his words:** "And here, the Garden of Eden is where we will enter paradise." [Dante 12 · 1:43:08](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6188)

### Leah and Rachel

*Who:* The sisters Jacob married in Genesis (see Chapter 2). Near the summit (Purgatory 27), Dante dreams of Leah gathering flowers, who says her sister Rachel sits at her mirror all day. Commentators traditionally read them as the active life and the contemplative life. Rachel sits beside Beatrice in the celestial rose.

**What he teaches**
- Dante's three dreams in Purgatory (the eagle, the siren, and Leah and Rachel) are not independent visions but "manifestations of his desires" [Dante 12 · 1:24:30](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=5070).
- The desire is for a wedding. Dante casts himself as "a new Jacob" about to marry his Rachel, Beatrice, after long labor [Dante 12 · 1:26:20](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=5180).
- The telling detail is Dante's own addition: "never in the Bible" does Rachel look in a mirror. What that detail slips into the dream is narcissism [Dante 12 · 1:28:37](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=5317); [Dante 12 · 1:28:46](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=5326).
- So the dream gives away a hidden motive: Dante expects Beatrice to praise him. She scolds him instead, telling him this is only the beginning and that someone "still stuck on fame" can do nothing in heaven [Dante 12 · 3:15:02](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=11702).
- In heaven, Rachel (like Beatrice) is honored for the happiness she created on earth, which heaven cannot produce by itself [Dante 5 · 2:44:05](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=9845).

**In his big picture:** The dream shows Jiang's method: an emotion hidden inside an image reveals more than what the dreamer consciously intends.

**In his words:** "what he really wants is Beatatrice to praise him to the sky" [Dante 12 · 1:30:32](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=5432)

### Saint Lucy (Lucia)

*Who:* Saint Lucy of Syracuse, an early-fourth-century virgin martyr and patron saint of eyesight. In the poem she sends Beatrice to help Dante (Inferno 2), carries him in his sleep to Purgatory's gate (Purgatory 9), and sits in the celestial rose.

**What he teaches**
- Dante dreams that an eagle snatches him. Virgil explains that Lucia came down from heaven and carried him to the gate [Dante 10 · 3:12:06](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=11526).
- The dream's emotion is fear (a student links it to Ganymede, carried off by an eagle). Why fear, if a saint is helping? Jiang offers a "life lesson": when a partner who should be angry is suddenly kind, you're in real trouble. After days of preening over his shadow, Dante senses that Beatrice's circle has stepped in [Dante 10 · 3:24:32](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=12272); [Dante 10 · 3:25:08](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=12308).
- He calls Lucia "the inverse" of Piccarda: Piccarda lacks will, while Lucia embodies it [Dante 5 · 2:21:04](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=8464). He tells her legend as a Christian who killed herself rather than be forced into marriage [Dante 10 · 3:20:38](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=12038).

**In his big picture:** Lucia is one of the women Dante raises to the top of heaven, and a symbol of will.

**In his words:** "The emotion in the dream is fear." [Dante 10 · 3:23:30](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=12210)

**Reality check:** According to tradition, Lucy was executed as a martyr under Diocletian after refusing marriage. The legends do not say she killed herself.

### Piccarda Donati

*Who:* A Florentine noblewoman and Poor Clare nun, sister of Forese and Corso Donati. Corso dragged her out of the convent to marry her off for political advantage. Dante meets her in the Moon, the lowest heaven, among souls who broke their vows (Paradise 3–4).

**What he teaches**
- The lowest sphere holds the "inconstant", people who failed to keep their vows. Piccarda is someone Dante knew in real life [Dante 1 · 2:26:46](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=8806); [Dante 1 · 2:27:24](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=8844).
- She says she is content where God put her. Jiang calls this a paradox: if all souls simply obey, why is there a hierarchy at all [Dante 1 · 2:32:01](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=9121)? Her real motive, he says, is fear, and the contentment is a pretext [Dante 1 · 2:49:39](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=10179).
- He runs a thought experiment: a fearless Piccarda stands on the convent roof and threatens to jump. The soldiers realize "You're fighting God now" and go home [Dante 1 · 2:51:12](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=10272); [Dante 1 · 2:52:43](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=10363). The point is that "your actions that define who you are" [Dante 1 · 2:50:51](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=10251).
- Beatrice's logic is strict. A vow hands your free will back to God, so breaking it is not a lapse. "She betrayed God" [Dante 1 · 3:41:06](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=13266).
- She illustrates two kinds of will. Her *absolute* will stayed with God, while her *contingent* will gave in to force [Dante 10 · 2:24:41](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8681).

**In his big picture:** Piccarda shows that goodness without courage is not enough. Fear, even in a saint, limits how close one comes to God.

**In his words:** "God gave Picarda free will and then Picarda gave her free will to God so that so that now they are eternally married." [Dante 1 · 3:40:37](https://youtu.be/7AG3EMDvKT0?t=13237)

### Justinian

*Who:* Byzantine emperor Justinian I (r. 527–565), who codified Roman law and whose general Belisarius reconquered Italy (his reign is in Chapter 5). In Paradise 5–7 he speaks from Mercury, the sphere of those who did good for the sake of honor, and tells the history of the Roman eagle.

**What he teaches**
- Before Justinian speaks, Beatrice tells Dante to trust these souls "as gods". Jiang flags that plural as a word that shouldn't be in a Christian heaven, and as evidence of the poem's "subversive quality" [Dante 2 · 1:36:06](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=5766); [Dante 2 · 1:38:50](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=5930).
- Justinian's speech is "the official chronology as presented by the Catholic Church" [Dante 2 · 1:39:35](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=5975). The eagle passes from Aeneas to Caesar, Titus and Charlemagne, and "the tone is that this is all pre-ordained by God" [Dante 2 · 2:38:39](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9519). Jiang notes that the Church taught Titus's destruction of the Temple as vengeance on the Jews [Dante 2 · 2:41:27](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9687).
- He notes that Justinian's wars, led by Belisarius, reunited the empire and imposed the Trinity [Dante 2 · 2:35:58](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9358). He reads Justinian's condemnation of Guelphs and Ghibellines alike, who both exploit Rome's emblem, as Dante's verdict on the Church's politics [Dante 2 · 2:42:04](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9724).
- Love aimed at fame reaches God "with lesser force" [Dante 2 · 2:44:28](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9868). Romeo, a just minister who died poor and exiled, stands in for Dante himself [Dante 2 · 2:46:33](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9993).
- In his view, Justinian's account is "basically just the same thing" as Anchises' imperial prophecy in the *Aeneid* [Dante 3 · 2:02:51](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7371).

**In his big picture:** Justinian is the voice of the Virgilian, imperial story of Providence, which Dante lets him tell in full before overturning it.

**In his words:** "The church should not be involved in temporal politics." [Dante 2 · 2:43:37](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=9817)

### Thomas Aquinas and Bonaventure

*Who:* Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), Dominican theologian, and Bonaventure (1221–1274), Franciscan minister general. In the Sun (Paradise 10–12), Thomas praises Francis of Assisi and Bonaventure praises Dominic, and each laments the decline of his own order. The historical significance of all four is in Chapter 5.

**What he teaches**
- Both orders were reform movements that brought hope to a corrupt Church, "But ultimately both fail." Dante's question is why [Dante 3 · 45:05](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=2705); [Dante 3 · 45:22](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=2722).
- The canto opens by attacking "syllogistic reasonings", learning pursued for power instead of truth. Jiang adds that it's "no different today" [Dante 3 · 46:55](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=2815); [Dante 3 · 47:26](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=2846).
- The Church is Christ's bride, and providence gives her "two princes". Francis marries Poverty as her second husband after Jesus, and Dominic marries faith [Dante 3 · 50:51](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=3051); [Dante 3 · 55:09](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=3309); [Dante 3 · 1:28:27](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=5307). Each speaker praises the other order and criticizes his own [Dante 3 · 48:19](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=2899).
- Jiang's diagnosis is that the Franciscans were ruined by gifts of property and the Dominicans, "the pre- Jesuits", by the power they gained as inquisitors [Dante 3 · 1:24:35](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=5075).
- The Dominicans were created to deal with the Cathars, Gnostics "who believe in the ideas that Dante believes" and who did not fear death. "Rather than use the sword, they use the pen": interrogation and re-education [Dante 3 · 1:31:12](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=5472); [Dante 3 · 1:33:55](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=5635).

**In his big picture:** Good founders cannot protect a movement from money and power, so reform from inside the Church fails. For Jiang, that is why Dante looks elsewhere, to the individual imagination.

**In his words:** "The Franciscans become corrupt because people want to give them property." [Dante 3 · 1:38:21](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=5901)

**Reality check:** The campaign against the Cathars did involve killing: the Albigensian Crusade, and inquisitors (many of them Dominicans) handing relapsed heretics to secular authorities to be burned. Jiang's claim that Aquinas was "the first to attempt" to reconcile Greek philosophy with Christianity [Dante 3 · 43:30](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=2610) overlooks predecessors such as Boethius and his own teacher, Albert the Great.

### Cacciaguida

*Who:* Dante's great-great-grandfather, a Florentine knight who died on the Second Crusade (c. 1147). In Mars (Paradise 15–17) he praises the old Florence and foretells Dante's exile.

**What he teaches**
- The "central conflict" of these cantos is how Dante can square his divine mission as a prophet-poet with the fact that "his life really sucks": exiled, stripped of his property, dependent on others [Dante 3 · 1:58:22](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7102).
- The first comfort is family history, which exile cannot take away [Dante 3 · 1:50:17](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=6617). The literary trick is that Dante set the journey in 1300 but wrote later, so the ancestor can "predict" the exile of 1302 [Dante 3 · 1:55:01](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=6901).
- Jiang sees Dante rewriting the *Aeneid*. In the underworld, Anchises shows Aeneas the triumph of Rome. Cacciaguida tells Dante how salty the bread of others tastes, "the complete opposite" [Dante 3 · 2:04:10](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7450); [Dante 3 · 2:09:12](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7752). The pope is the one plotting it [Dante 3 · 2:08:28](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7708).
- Cacciaguida tells Dante to quit his faction, so exile also means isolation [Dante 3 · 2:12:09](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7929). The praise of the Veronese lords who will shelter him is, Jiang jokes, "just sucking up to his patron" [Dante 3 · 2:14:54](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=8094).
- Even so, the bleak prophecy gives Dante purpose "just like Inas" gets from his [Dante 3 · 2:19:25](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=8365).

**In his big picture:** Cacciaguida turns the imperial prophecy of glory into a prophecy of creative exile. Suffering and solitude are the price of real creativity.

**In his words:** "He appreciates that his purpose is to go into exile and isolation in order to write this divine comedy." [Dante 3 · 2:22:23](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=8543)

### Ripheus and Trajan

*Who:* Ripheus is a minor Trojan warrior whom Virgil calls "most just" in *Aeneid* 2. Trajan (r. 98–117) is the Roman emperor whom medieval legend says Pope Gregory the Great's prayers raised from the dead long enough to be baptized. Dante places both in the eye of the Eagle of Justice in Jupiter (Paradise 20).

**What he teaches**
- He brings in Paradise 20 during a Purgatory session, right after Statius. The Eagle's eye holds David (unsurprising) and Trajan, who lived after Christ and so could have converted [Dante 11 · 3:19:26](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=11966).
- Ripheus is "a huge paradox". A Trojan born before both the Hebrews and Jesus, he could not have known about God, yet he is in heaven [Dante 11 · 3:21:24](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12084).
- When a student says you can follow your intuition and just be a good person, Jiang answers "Exactly" [Dante 11 · 3:22:32](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12152). He then asks why Virgil clings so hard to the Christians-only rule.

**In his big picture:** Ripheus is the strongest case against Virgil's framework: a pre-Christian pagan saved by goodness alone.

**In his words:** "It can really counteracts the point that Virgil makes which is like you have to be a Christian in order to make it to heaven." [Dante 11 · 3:22:39](https://youtu.be/oR3EOW52WCc?t=12159)

**Reality check:** In the same canto, the Eagle explains both cases inside Christian doctrine. Trajan was briefly revived and converted, and Ripheus was granted faith in the Redeemer to come, baptized by faith, hope and charity rather than by water. Dante keeps faith in Christ as the condition of salvation.

### Saints Peter, James and John

*Who:* The three apostles closest to Jesus. In the heaven of the fixed stars (Paradise 24–27), Peter examines Dante on faith, James on hope and John on love, and John's brightness briefly blinds him. Peter's life is in Chapter 5.

**What he teaches**
- He frames the scene as a doctoral exam: Dante is "tested by Peter, James, and John" in an academic setting approved by the Church. It looks pious on the surface, but "he's heretical" [Dante 4 · 20:13](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=1213); [Dante 4 · 20:35](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=1235).
- Asked for his creed, Dante should have "recited the Nicene Creed". Instead he says God is love, which Jiang calls "heresy" [Dante 4 · 43:36](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=2616). Jiang argues that the Trinity exists to create mystery, and "mystery is the foundation of Catholic Church". You "can't write a story where you meet the Holy Trinity", so Dante reduces its "one essence" to love [Dante 4 · 50:39](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=3039); [Dante 4 · 51:10](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=3070).
- On hope, Beatrice answers for Dante that no Christian has more of it. Jiang sees "the arrogance of Dante": his hope is to write the poem, and it is presented as sanctioned by heaven [Dante 4 · 1:10:44](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=4244); [Dante 4 · 1:11:13](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=4273). Only Christ and Mary rose to heaven in the body, yet Dante is there alive and in the body [Dante 4 · 1:19:37](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=4777).
- Blinded by John, Dante must see "with your third eye" [Dante 4 · 1:26:51](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=5211).
- Peter's rant against the man who "usurps my place" means "clearly ... the Pope is the Antichrist" [Dante 4 · 1:56:04](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=6964). Dante got away with it, he says, because the Church was split between Rome and Avignon [Dante 4 · 22:16](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=1336).

**In his big picture:** Dante uses the Church's own founders to certify his doctrine of love and to condemn the papacy.

**In his words:** "The essence of everything is love." [Dante 4 · 52:45](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=3165)

**Reality check:** "God is love" is scripture (1 John 4:8) and orthodox Catholic teaching, and Dante's creed in canto 24 explicitly professes the Trinity. In the poem it is John, not James, who denies having risen bodily. In Dante's lifetime there was only one pope. The papacy moved to Avignon under French pressure, and rival popes appeared only with the Great Schism of 1378.

### Adam

*Who:* The first man (for Genesis, see Chapter 2). In Paradise 26 he answers Dante's unspoken questions about his creation, his time in Eden, the real cause of the Fall and the language he spoke.

**What he teaches**
- Adam says his exile came not from tasting the fruit but from "trespass of the boundary". Jiang reads this as Dante making Adam, not Eve, responsible, and calls it "revolutionary" [Dante 4 · 1:36:37](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=5797); [Dante 4 · 1:37:02](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=5822).
- It fits a pattern. Women are raised up in Dante's heaven: Beatrice teaches, and Mary is "elevated higher than Jesus". Jiang calls this "a social critique" of how the Church oppressed women, since love requires "equality between the sexes" [Dante 4 · 1:37:41](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=5861); [Dante 4 · 1:41:09](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=6069); [Dante 4 · 1:41:16](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=6076).
- Adam says even God's name changed and every language changes. The Church insists faith is immutable, but Adam says "it's all change"; what matters is "your connection to the divine" [Dante 4 · 1:45:24](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=6324); [Dante 4 · 1:45:35](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=6335).
- In an earlier lecture, Jiang set this against Augustine's reading, in which Adam fell out of love for Eve, so "love can only misguide you" [Civ 41 · 46:16](https://youtu.be/v6CDqJnrvLk?t=2776).

**In his big picture:** Dante's Adam takes back both love and women from the Augustinian blame for the Fall.

**In his words:** "This is saying that it's Adam and not Eve who was responsible for the original sin." [Dante 4 · 1:36:37](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=5797)

**Reality check:** Christian theology has long laid the Fall chiefly on Adam (Paul in Romans 5; Augustine). Adam's line is usually read as defining the sin as disobedience, overstepping God's limit, not as shifting blame from Eve. In Catholic teaching Mary is the highest of creatures but not above Christ.

### Bernard of Clairvaux

*Who:* Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153), Cistercian abbot, preacher of the Second Crusade and patron of the Knights Templar, famous for his devotion to Mary. He replaces Beatrice as Dante's final guide (Paradise 31–33).

**What he teaches**
- The switch comes at the climax, which is puzzling ("who the hell is Bernard?"). Jiang describes him as "almost like the first love poet" of the Catholic tradition, a co-founder of the Knights Templar who wrote love poetry "to Mary not Jesus not God" [Dante 5 · 1:12:58](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=4378); [Dante 5 · 1:13:04](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=4384); [Dante 5 · 1:13:58](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=4438).
- The standard explanation is that Mary is queen of heaven and Bernard is her servant [Dante 5 · 1:14:21](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=4461). Jiang's own logic goes further. Love means forgetting yourself, and that is how Beatrice got Dante up the mountain. But to meet God "You must express your ego", because the ego is the source of imagination, so Beatrice has to step aside [Dante 5 · 1:23:06](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=4986); [Dante 5 · 1:23:19](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=4999); [Dante 5 · 1:24:12](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=5052).
- In his prayer Bernard burns for Dante's vision more than his own. Jiang's reading is that the blessed have no bodies, and so no imagination: "Only Dante with his imagination can truly understand God" [Dante 5 · 2:29:12](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=8952); [GB 12 · 28:06](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1686).

**In his big picture:** Bernard is heaven admitting its limits. The saints live with God but only an embodied human can "see" God.

**In his words:** "We angels, we souls, we don't have an imagination." [Dante 5 · 2:28:28](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=8908)

**Reality check:** Bernard did not found the Templars (Hugues de Payens did, c. 1119). He won them church approval in 1129 and wrote in their praise. His works are sermons and treatises, not love poetry.

### The Virgin Mary

*Who:* Mary, mother of Jesus, whom Dante shows enthroned as queen of the celestial rose. Bernard's prayer to her opens the final canto, Paradise 33.

**What he teaches**
- In January 2025 he said that in Dante's heaven, "at the very center is Mary", not Jesus, and he posed the question: "is it a greater miracle that a mortal woman is able to give birth to a God" [Civ 29 · 05:12](https://youtu.be/GfqJi0qevok?t=312).
- Bernard's prayer is a string of paradoxes ("virgin mother, daughter of your son"). Jiang takes them as a rebuttal of Augustine. Mary's nobility lies in "redeeming yourself from sin" through love, a mother's love [Civ 29 · 17:22](https://youtu.be/GfqJi0qevok?t=1042); [Civ 29 · 18:57](https://youtu.be/GfqJi0qevok?t=1137).
- By 2026: Mary is the portal through which God entered the world, and Dante's mission completes one "that Mary first started" [GB 12 · 26:36](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1596). In the seminar he called Bernard's prayer "such a revolutionary passage" [Dante 5 · 2:25:06](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=8706).

**In his big picture:** Mary is the human who first "expanded the possibility of humanity" by containing God, and the model for Dante's own mission.

**In his words:** "So God was able to come into our world through the portal that is Mary." [GB 12 · 26:19](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1579)

**Reality check:** Dante's Mary, highest of creatures and channel of grace, reflects mainstream medieval devotion, Bernard's included. In the poem she is still "more humble and sublime than any creature", below God.

### God (the final vision)

*Who:* In Paradise 33, Dante looks into the divine light. He sees the universe "bound by love" into one volume, then three circles (the Trinity), one of them bearing a human image, and finally his will is turned by "the love that moves the sun and the other stars".

**What he teaches**
- Dante makes the journey because "God wants to meet him". God is perfect and knows everything, so God "cannot know itself" and has no imagination. God created humanity to know itself and chose Dante [GB 12 · 21:51](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1311); [GB 12 · 22:35](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1355). He made the same claim in January 2025 [Civ 29 · 15:19](https://youtu.be/GfqJi0qevok?t=919).
- Only a human can do this, because the soul connects to the source while the body brings limits, pain and pleasure, which is where imagination comes from: "only Dante can tell God what it is" [GB 12 · 24:34](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1474).
- The vision first shows "the totality of the universe". In 2025 he compared it to the universe before the Big Bang [GB 12 · 32:34](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1954); [Civ 29 · 22:59](https://youtu.be/GfqJi0qevok?t=1379). Then, inside the Trinity, a human likeness appears: "we are inside it at its very heart". The poem "ends with a question": why are we inside God [GB 12 · 36:54](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=2214); [GB 12 · 38:04](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=2284)?
- His answer is that the image echoes Beatrice's three-mirror experiment in Paradise 2: the candle reflected into infinity is the candle burning in us [GB 12 · 40:20](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=2420). In the seminar he adds that "Earth and heaven have to be combined together" because some forms of happiness can only be created on earth [Dante 5 · 2:42:20](https://youtu.be/xPxEi5Zm0G4?t=9740).
- The poem is Dante's reconstruction of the vision twenty years later, and his mission is "to report to us what he saw" [GB 12 · 31:59](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1919).

**In his big picture:** The final vision rounds off his theology of imagination. God and humanity complete each other, and love is the force that binds the universe.

**In his words:** "God is love. You want to know what God is? Love someone." [GB 12 · 41:27](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=2487)

**Reality check:** In lines Jiang read aloud, the poem calls God "self-knowing, self-known" [GB 12 · 35:47](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=2147), which cuts against the claim that God cannot know itself. The human image in the second circle is traditionally read as Christ's human nature (the Incarnation), not as humanity in general.

- **Casella** (Florentine musician and friend of Dante): his love song holds the newly arrived souls until Cato drives them on. Jiang contrasts this warm reunion with the frosty Virgil–Cato meeting [Dante 9 · 2:56:35](https://youtu.be/SSmJKnUOsAc?t=10595).

## Key ideas from these lectures

- **Paradox as Dante's method**: every oddity (a pagan guardian, a disciple above his master, a pagan Trojan in heaven) is a puzzle meant to make the reader think. "The Divine Comedy is nothing but paradoxes" [GB 11 · 55:31](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=3331); [Civ 29 · 06:20](https://youtu.be/GfqJi0qevok?t=380).
- **Hell versus Purgatory**: both are chosen. Hell is for those who "refuse to admit you've committed a sin". Purgatory is for those willing to do penance, however long it takes [Civ 30 · 36:06](https://youtu.be/asbKjezwIJM?t=2166).
- **Self-forgiveness and the seven P's**: an angel marks seven P's (for *peccatum*, "sin") on Dante's forehead, and one is removed at each terrace [GB 11 · 08:18](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=498). What slows souls down is not God's anger but their own unwillingness to forgive themselves [GB 11 · 34:43](https://youtu.be/otyUpKhpTYM?t=2083).
- **Pray, don't pay**: Dante names real people in Purgatory so their families will pray for them instead of paying bribes to a corrupt Church [Dante 10 · 2:01:55](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=7315); [Dante 10 · 2:03:14](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=7394).
- **Absolute and contingent will**: what you truly are and believe, versus what you choose under pressure. Life's task is aligning the two [Dante 10 · 2:24:23](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=8663).
- **Sin as trauma**: wrongdoing distorts the soul the way a wound hurts the body, so its memory has to be washed away [Dante 12 · 1:52:50](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=6770).
- **Just indignation**: anger that comes from love (Beatrice's, Peter's, Dante's at Florence) is legitimate, and it is what drove Dante to write [Dante 12 · 3:19:46](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=11986); [Dante 12 · 3:53:53](https://youtu.be/Sa5kffYLvBE?t=14033).
- **Hell first, then heaven**: you have to see for yourself that hatred leads nowhere before you can rise [Dante 3 · 2:13:55](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=8035). Likewise "for us to understand, we first have to misunderstand" [GB 10 · 46:41](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2801).
- **Higher vision**: seeing God means using the imagination, not the eyes. "Higher vision still is the imagination" [GB 12 · 27:12](https://youtu.be/FspDllFoiDE?t=1632).
- **Cognitive dissonance**: his explanation for how Virgil can ignore what he saw of Cato, and how Catholic readers can miss the poem's heresies [Dante 10 · 2:31:00](https://youtu.be/MDOK9qaaA7Q?t=9060); [Dante 4 · 19:56](https://youtu.be/brRNCLD7to0?t=1196).
- **A subversive text**: you can read the *Comedy* as a Christian poem, and most people do, but small details "subvert the traditional understanding of Christianity" [Dante 2 · 1:39:13](https://youtu.be/eF5B3CWpKxg?t=5953).
