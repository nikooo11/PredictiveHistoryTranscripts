# Chapter 4: Rome

*Mainly draws on Civilization #14–17 (Nov 2024) and #32 (Feb 2025), Secret History #21 (Nov 2025) and Great Books #7–8 (Mar 2026), with supporting passages from other lectures.*

## The story he tells

Jiang's Rome is a war machine born in a poor, hilly borderland where everyone had to fight [SH 21 · 01:20](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=80). He doesn't explain its rise by manpower, technology or money. He explains it by "character", meaning three values. Piety was duty to the gods, the city and your father. Liberty was obedience to Rome's laws, history and institutions, and above all it meant no kings. *Res publica*, public virtue, was a lifelong competition to serve Rome [Civ 14 · 28:42](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1722) [Civ 14 · 29:42](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1782). These values made Romans cohesive, disciplined and devoted, so Rome could lose battle after battle to Hannibal and still win the war [Civ 14 · 23:01](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1381). Rome also "created history as mythology": founding legends like those of Romulus and Lucius Brutus were taught as a civic religion [Civ 14 · 33:32](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2012).

Victory broke the system. Once Rome ruled the Mediterranean it became a contradiction, an "imperial republic", and its aggression turned inward: inequality, the murdered Gracchi, Marius and Sulla, then Caesar [Civ 15 · 10:37](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=637). Caesar rises as a "mythmaker". He is killed because his new myth unsettles the old one [Civ 15 · 04:44](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=284). His death turns his myth into reality and carries Octavian to power [Civ 16 · 15:06](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=906). As Augustus, Octavian has to replace liberty with piety and Homer with a Roman epic. So he sponsors Virgil's Aeneid, "the greatest work of propaganda ever in human history" [Civ 17 · 13:21](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=801).

His tone darkens over time. In 2024 he admires how Rome's values beat Carthage, while still calling the Romans "a repugnant people" [Civ 14 · 1:12:16](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=4336). By November 2025 Rome is the great anti-civilization, "the evil empire", and its history is mostly invented [SH 21 · 00:01](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1). By 2026 the Aeneid is the "anti-Homer", a poison that shaped a thousand years until Dante supplied the antidote [GB 7 · 03:36](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=216). All along, Rome is a mirror for America: a republic that modelled itself on Rome and, he argues, will turn its violence inward too [Civ 32 · 00:00](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=0).

## The main figures

### Aeneas

*Who:* A Trojan prince of legend, son of the goddess Venus and the mortal Anchises. In Virgil's Aeneid he escapes burning Troy with his father and son, loses his wife Creusa, loves and leaves Dido in Carthage, and wins a foothold in Italy from which Rome's founders descend.

**What he teaches**
- **An ancestor for the Caesars.** Augustus needed legitimacy, and his family, the Julii, came from neither of Rome's founding myths (Romulus and Lucius Brutus). So, in Jiang's telling, Julius Caesar "planted the seeds" of a myth that both the Julii and Romulus descend from Aeneas, which makes the Julii Rome's first family [Civ 17 · 06:45](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=405) [Civ 17 · 07:52](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=472).
- **The night Troy falls.** Aeneas wants to kill Helen for causing the war until his mother Venus stops him. Then he wants to die fighting, until a flame crowns his son Iulus's head and his father reads it as a sign that the boy will found an empire [Civ 17 · 45:19](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2719) [Civ 17 · 47:19](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2839). He carries his father on his back and leads his son while his wife follows behind. Jiang reads this as the Roman order of priority: patriarch, then heir, then wife [GB 7 · 25:29](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=1529).
- **Creusa.** Jiang says Creusa kills herself so that her husband can marry into a new royal line, and so that she won't shame him as a Greek slave. His summary of the poem's message: "a good wife is someone who will kill herself for her husband" [Civ 17 · 48:07](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2887) [GB 7 · 26:04](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=1564).
- **Leaving Dido.** When Mercury orders him on, Aeneas's only dilemma is how to slip away without a scene. "There's nothing human about Inas", Jiang says; he's "like James Bond" [GB 8 · 10:11](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=611) [GB 8 · 10:35](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=635). He reads Aeneas's reply to Dido as: what matters is power, and love is in the way [GB 8 · 22:43](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1363).
- **The prophecy.** In the underworld, his dead father's prophecy "becomes the history of Rome": conquest of Carthage, Julius Caesar, Augustus and the Pax Romana [Dante 3 · 2:02:23](https://youtu.be/6tzAyCZ4Omk?t=7343).
- **An arc toward piety.** At Troy and in Carthage the gods had to step in to keep him on his mission. At the end he kills the pleading Turnus with no god prompting him. That, Jiang argues, is the epiphany: he "has become the embodiment of piety and Duty" [Civ 17 · 53:09](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3189) [GB 8 · 44:34](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2674).

**In his big picture:** Aeneas is the anti-Achilles and anti-Odysseus (see Chapter 3). Homer's heroes are healed by love; Aeneas is perfected by giving it up. By memorizing the poem, Roman schoolboys turned from humans into "basically a robot" [GB 8 · 46:04](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2764).

**In his words:** "There's nothing human about Inas." [GB 8 · 10:35](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=635)

**Reality check:** In Virgil's text Creusa is lost during the escape, and her ghost says the gods keep her at Troy; the suicide is Jiang's reading. The legend of Aeneas in Italy and the Julian family's claim to descend from his son both predate Caesar, who publicized the claim rather than inventing it.

### Dido

*Who:* The legendary Phoenician queen who founded Carthage after fleeing Tyre. In Book 4 of the Aeneid she falls in love with Aeneas. When he leaves at the gods' command, she curses his descendants and kills herself on a pyre.

**What he teaches**
- **Seduced by a storyteller.** Dido falls for Aeneas partly because he tells the fall of Troy so beautifully. Juno engineers their union to stop Rome being born, and Jupiter sends Mercury to break it up [GB 8 · 04:54](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=294) [GB 8 · 05:43](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=343).
- **The broken pledge.** Her plea about "the pledge once sealed with our right hands" echoes, for Jiang, the keepsake that proves Odysseus's identity to Penelope. Homer's love-bond survives twenty years; for Aeneas it means nothing [GB 8 · 14:52](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=892). The Odyssey ends in love at home, while the Aeneid begins in love and demands that you abandon it [GB 8 · 17:02](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1022).
- **Love as disintegration.** A proud queen sinks to wondering whether to beg a place on the Trojan ships as a slave girl. In Homer love gives strength; here, "by falling in love, you disintegrate as a person" [GB 8 · 25:42](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1542) [GB 8 · 26:40](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1600). Meanwhile Aeneas sleeps peacefully, "meaning he doesn't care" [GB 8 · 27:34](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1654). In 2024 he put the poem's message bluntly: "love is a disease" [Civ 17 · 48:57](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2937).
- **The curse as war propaganda.** Dying, she calls for endless war between the two peoples. For Jiang this is Virgil explaining Hannibal and excusing Rome's annihilation of Carthage, since the Carthaginians were cursed to avenge her [Civ 17 · 49:51](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2991) [GB 8 · 31:28](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1888).
- **A heroine turned inside out.** In the Carthaginian version, he says, Dido killed herself rather than marry a local warlord, and that sacrifice made Carthaginians "a free and proud people". Virgil inverts her into a woman poisoned by love who enslaves her people to revenge [GB 8 · 31:56](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1916) [GB 8 · 32:35](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1955).
- **Her afterlife.** In Dante's Hell, Virgil names a thousand lovers but not Dido, his own creation. Jiang speculates that a real woman behind Dido had spurned Virgil, and that Dante names her out of mercy (see Chapter 6) [Civ 30 · 15:37](https://youtu.be/asbKjezwIJM?t=937) [Civ 30 · 17:57](https://youtu.be/asbKjezwIJM?t=1077).

**In his big picture:** Dido is his main evidence that the Aeneid turns Homer's creed, love as the unifying force of the universe, into its opposite. He hears the same distrust of love later in Augustine (see Chapter 5) [Civ 27 · 34:17](https://youtu.be/GdbZBVUIVDE?t=2057).

**In his words:** "by falling in love, you disintegrate as a person" [GB 8 · 25:42](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=1542)

**Reality check:** Dido is a figure of legend, set centuries before Virgil's time. There is no evidence for a real woman who rejected the poet.

### Turnus

*Who:* Prince of the Rutulians in the Aeneid, betrothed to Lavinia, daughter of King Latinus, before Aeneas arrived. He leads the Italian war against the Trojans and is killed by Aeneas in the poem's final lines.

**What he teaches**
- **How the war starts.** Latinus is so impressed by Aeneas that he offers him his daughter, but the queen has already promised her to Turnus, and war follows [Civ 17 · 49:51](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2991) [GB 8 · 34:59](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2099).
- **The Iliad replayed in reverse.** The final duel is Achilles against Hector with the outcome flipped. Turnus even begs in Priam's words to Achilles, asking Aeneas to think of his own father. Virgil is "plagiarizing Homer", Jiang says, in order to "invert and subvert Homer" [GB 8 · 37:32](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2252) [GB 8 · 38:16](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2296).
- **Pallas's belt.** Aeneas is about to spare him when he sees the sword belt Turnus stripped from young Pallas, Aeneas's friend and the poem's Patroclus, and he kills Turnus in a rage [GB 8 · 39:54](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2394) [Civ 17 · 51:57](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3117).
- **The ending is finished, not abrupt.** Scholars call the ending too sudden, even unfinished. Jiang says they are misreading political propaganda: the epiphany is that Aeneas overrides his own pity with no god telling him to [GB 8 · 42:03](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2523) [GB 8 · 44:34](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2674).
- **A student pushes back.** A student argues that Aeneas acts out of love for Pallas. Jiang answers that true love forgives, as Achilles learns with Priam, and that using a dead friend as an excuse to kill is not love [GB 8 · 47:17](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2837) [GB 8 · 48:03](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2883).

**In his big picture:** Turnus's death completes Virgil's "perfect soldier", who has thrown away pity, decency and love for obedience [GB 8 · 44:56](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2696).

**In his words:** "But in this battle, it is Hector who wins and Achilles is basically begging for mercy." [GB 8 · 37:32](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=2252)

### Virgil

*Who:* Publius Vergilius Maro (70–19 BC), the leading poet of Augustus's Rome, author of the Eclogues, the Georgics and the Aeneid, which was unfinished at his death. His role as Dante's guide is covered in Chapter 6.

**What he teaches**
- **The commissioned poet.** Augustus invited Virgil, the greatest living Roman poet, to write a Latin epic that would replace Homer in the schools. From 29 to 19 BC he worked "under direct supervision" of Augustus, so they were "basically co-authors" [Civ 17 · 12:11](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=731) [Civ 17 · 13:21](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=801). By 2026 Jiang goes further: "it was actually Augustus Caesar who wrote this", with Virgil putting Augustus's framework into verse and reading him each draft [GB 7 · 08:22](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=502) [SH 21 · 1:03:17](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3797).
- **Three jobs in one poem.** The Aeneid had to make the Julii Rome's first family, put piety above liberty, and brand Greek culture as corrupt and hedonistic [Civ 17 · 13:21](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=801). The Greek spy's moving lie about the wooden horse gives the game away: "the real children horse is Greek culture", meaning logic, philosophy and theatre [Civ 17 · 43:01](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2581) [GB 7 · 08:05](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=485).
- **The anti-Homer.** Virgil inverts Homer point by point. Priam's generosity becomes a foolish trust that dooms Troy. Priam's murder at the altar by Achilles's son cancels the Iliad's lesson of forgiving your enemy (see Chapter 3) [GB 7 · 13:55](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=835) [GB 7 · 16:12](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=972). Love, Homer's unifying force, becomes for Virgil the source of conflict, and piety is the cure [Civ 17 · 56:15](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3375) [GB 8 · 00:33](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=33).
- **Hate at the centre.** In SH 21 he says Augustus asked Virgil to put "hate at the center of the universe and not love", so that Roman schoolchildren would hate the Greeks. He calls the poem "a complete piece of crap" [SH 21 · 1:02:45](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3765) [SH 21 · 1:03:13](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3793) [SH 21 · 1:07:55](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4075).
- **The deathbed request.** Virgil asked for the Aeneid to be burned. Jiang argues that a poet who used his divine gift to serve an emperor and spread hatred feared punishment. Augustus refused [GB 7 · 08:55](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=535) [SH 21 · 1:03:47](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3827).
- **A thousand years of influence.** Memorized by every elite child, it became "the Bible of the Roman Empire". Through the Dark Ages, "Virgil becomes the god of the Catholic Church", until Dante [GB 7 · 09:13](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=553) [GB 8 · 02:14](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=134).

**In his big picture:** Virgil is his prime example of the poet as propagandist, the opposite of the poet-prophets (Homer, Dante) he ranks as history's greatest figures [SH 21 · 1:13:12](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4392). He teaches the book because it shaped the West, while telling students it "is not a great book" [GB 1 · 43:50](https://youtu.be/TsD-8FGA84A?t=2630).

**In his words:** "Homer is an educator whereas Virgil is a propagandist" [Civ 17 · 15:27](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=927)

**Reality check:** The Aeneid has twelve books, not the twenty-four he gives in GB 7–8; the first six echo the Odyssey and the last six the Iliad. Augustus was Virgil's patron and followed the work closely, but "co-author" is Jiang's interpretation. The ancient biographies say Virgil wanted the poem burned because it was unfinished; the motive Jiang gives is his own.

### Romulus

*Who:* The legendary founder and first king of Rome (traditionally 753 BC). He was the son of the god Mars and a priestess, and he and his twin Remus were nursed by a she-wolf. He killed Remus and named the city after himself.

**What he teaches**
- **The founding legend.** A usurper locks the old king's daughter in a temple as a virgin. She bears twins by Mars, whom the usurper orders killed; instead they are nursed by a she-wolf, raised by a shepherd, and later restore their grandfather to the throne [Civ 14 · 34:41](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2081).
- **A fratricide at the root.** Only one twin can be king, so Romulus kills Remus. From this Jiang infers a Roman psychology in which "violence is at the core of human existence", and killing your own brother can be right [Civ 14 · 36:34](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2194). After reading Livy's version in class, he concludes: "Rome was founded on violence" [SH 21 · 40:15](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=2415).
- **A nation of immigrants.** Romulus welcomed newcomers and gave them citizenship at once. This open citizenship later let Rome refill its armies in a way its rivals couldn't [Civ 14 · 36:34](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2194) [Civ 14 · 37:32](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2252).
- **The Sabine women.** When neighbours refuse to let their daughters marry Romans, Romulus stages a festival and the Romans abduct and rape the girls. He promises them citizenship and protection, and the women stop the war that follows. The lesson: "it's a cult of Rome all that matters is survival of Rome morals don't matter" [Civ 14 · 38:20](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2300) [Civ 14 · 39:11](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2351). In SH 21 he calls Livy's excuses "pretty disgusting" and reads the tale as a metaphor for conquest: winners kill the men, take the women, and write the history [SH 21 · 44:26](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=2666) [SH 21 · 1:21:37](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4897).
- **Recycled myth.** He treats the twin story as an older steppe (Proto-Indo-European) myth of a man sacrificing his twin, adapted to Rome [SH 21 · 1:01:10](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3670).

**In his big picture:** Romulus illustrates his rule that what a people believes about its origins matters more than what happened [Civ 14 · 33:32](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2012).

**In his words:** "Rome was founded on violence." [SH 21 · 40:15](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=2415)

### Lucius Junius Brutus

*Who:* The legendary founder of the Roman Republic (traditionally 509 BC). A nephew of the last king, Tarquin the Proud, he led the revolt after the king's son raped Lucretia, and became one of Rome's first two consuls.

**What he teaches**
- **Lucretia and the revolt.** The king's son rapes Lucretia, a woman famous for her virtue. She makes her husband and his friend Brutus swear revenge and stabs herself. Brutus rallies the nobility and they lock the king out of Rome [Civ 14 · 41:10](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2470) [Civ 14 · 41:59](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2519).
- **Dividing the king.** In Jiang's account Brutus split the king's five powers (military, judicial, legislative, administrative and religious) among elected institutions: consul, praetor, Senate, aedile and *pontifex maximus*. He calls this "the heart of republicanism", largely copied from the Etruscans [Civ 14 · 42:53](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2573) [Civ 14 · 45:45](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2745).
- **Executing his sons.** His two sons join a plot to bring back the king. As consul, Brutus presides over their beheading in front of all Rome, weeping but unflinching. Devotion means being "willing to sacrifice your own children" for Rome [Civ 14 · 46:42](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2802) [Civ 14 · 48:31](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2911).
- **Death and legacy.** He and the king's son spear each other in single combat. He was remembered as the greatest Roman until Julius Caesar, and it was "in the memory and honor" of him that Caesar would be assassinated [Civ 14 · 49:29](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2969).
- **Trauma as fuel.** In SH 21 the execution becomes the key to Roman psychology. Guilt tears a hole in Brutus, and he turns its energy on the enemy: "Trauma is a drug" [SH 21 · 55:26](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3326). Haunted by his sons, he must keep killing, which Jiang calls "the Roman way" and the source of secret societies' power (see Chapter 10) [SH 21 · 1:19:48](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4788) [SH 21 · 1:20:00](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4800).
- **Probably legend.** He stresses that these stories, recorded by Livy under Augustus, are "probably not true"; what matters is that Romans believed them [Civ 14 · 1:00:12](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=3612) [Civ 14 · 1:02:05](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=3725).

**In his big picture:** Lucius Brutus's founding myth is the rival that Caesar's myth had to overcome. Marcus and Decimus Brutus saw themselves as his heirs, bound to kill would-be kings [Civ 16 · 16:15](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=975) [Civ 17 · 08:56](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=536).

**In his words:** "Trauma is a drug." [SH 21 · 55:26](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3326)

**Reality check:** Historians treat the story as legend, as he does. The offices he credits to Brutus actually developed over centuries; the praetorship, for example, dates from 367 BC.

### Hannibal Barca

*Who:* A Carthaginian general (247–c.183 BC) who crossed the Alps in the Second Punic War and destroyed Roman armies at Trebia, Lake Trasimene and Cannae (216 BC). Scipio finally defeated him at Zama (202 BC).

**What he teaches**
- **The greatest general, and a realist.** Considered by many "the greatest General who ever lived", Hannibal saw that Rome "will not stop until it has conquered the world", so he struck first [Civ 14 · 09:37](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=577) [Civ 14 · 10:54](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=654). Carthage's merchant Council of Elders thought war bad for business, so he paid them off with Spanish silver [Civ 14 · 11:58](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=718).
- **Cannae (the 2024 version).** After the Alps he meets a Roman army twice his size in a narrow valley. He lets his curved centre bend back and encircles the Romans with his cavalry, the "double envelopment". About 70,000 Romans die: a fifth of Rome's adult men and a third of the Senate [Civ 14 · 14:43](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=883) [Civ 14 · 16:35](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=995) [Civ 14 · 18:51](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1131).
- **Why he lost anyway.** The Senate rejects his peace offer, and Rome never fights him in open battle again. He has no supply line, so he must forage, and at home the Elders undercut him for fear he'll return as king [Civ 14 · 19:53](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1193) [Civ 14 · 1:05:36](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=3936) [Civ 14 · 1:06:27](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=3987). Thanks to its strategic depth, Rome loses almost every battle but wins the one that matters, at Zama [Civ 35 · 36:01](https://youtu.be/7k5XoLzwmls?t=2161).
- **A general, not a legislator.** Hannibal is his example of a brilliant soldier who lacked the politician's and lawmaker's gifts that Caesar combined [Civ 15 · 56:28](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3388).
- **Dido's avenger.** In Virgil, Hannibal fulfils Dido's curse, which Jiang reads as Rome's alibi for razing Carthage [Civ 17 · 49:51](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2991).
- **In 2025, "it didn't happen."** In SH 21 he applies his three tests (historical pattern, game theory and religion) and concludes that Cannae "was completely made up". His reasons: great generals make themselves kings, yet Hannibal sits in Italy for years; no archaeology has found the battlefield; and conquerors think they are sons of God, not avengers of their fathers. Hannibal, he suggests, may not have existed [SH 21 · 17:15](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1035) [SH 21 · 17:32](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1052) [SH 21 · 19:02](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1142). He claims the Greek hostage Polybius invented the story to justify destroying Carthage, and that "all Roman history is complete nonsense" [SH 21 · 20:06](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1206) [SH 21 · 21:30](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1290).

**In his big picture:** At first Hannibal proves that nations, not armies, win wars. Later he becomes a test case for Jiang's view that official history is propaganda written by the winners [SH 21 · 16:51](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1011).

**In his words:** "armies don't win Wars Nations win Wars" [Civ 14 · 1:06:27](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=3987)

**Reality check:** Historians accept Hannibal and Cannae as real. Polybius was not an official historian and wrote within living memory. He drew on survivors and on earlier accounts, including Greeks who travelled with Hannibal. The battlefield's exact location is debated, which is common for ancient battles, but that is no evidence the battle never happened.

### Julius Caesar

*Who:* Gaius Julius Caesar (100–44 BC), the Roman politician and general who conquered Gaul and crossed the Rubicon to win a civil war against Pompey and the Senate's conservatives. He ruled as dictator until he was assassinated on the Ides of March, 44 BC.

**What he teaches**
- **The mythmaker.** Caesar, "considered the greatest historical figure of all time", succeeded because he cast himself as the hero of a story he was writing and bent reality to fit it. Jiang compares Steve Jobs's reality distortion and Trump's "make America great again" [Civ 15 · 00:00](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=0) [Civ 15 · 00:47](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=47) [Civ 15 · 03:46](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=226). The pirate story shows the method: he demands a higher ransom, jokes that he'll crucify his captors, then does, or says he did. What mattered to him was not whether the facts were true but how appealing the details were [Civ 15 · 27:30](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1650) [Civ 15 · 28:19](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1699).
- **Born into a contradiction.** The "imperial republic" bred bribery, plunder of the provinces, landless poor and debt. The Gracchi were murdered, Marius and Sulla marched on Rome, and the teenage Caesar, Marius's nephew, was put on Sulla's death list and bribed his way off it [Civ 15 · 10:37](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=637) [Civ 15 · 24:33](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1473).
- **Gaul as a myth factory.** In Gaul he paid off his debts, bought popularity with "bread and circus", built an army loyal only to him, and sent dispatches to be read aloud in the Forum. Invading Britain was "basically equivalent back then of the moon landing" [Civ 15 · 36:12](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2172) [Civ 15 · 36:57](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2217) [Civ 15 · 38:47](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2327). By 2025 he stresses slaves: Caesar got rich by enslaving Gauls he claimed to have killed, and "a lot of these victories were made up" [SH 21 · 31:11](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1871) [SH 21 · 32:11](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=1931).
- **Civil war and clemency.** Facing prosecution, he crosses the Rubicon. His veterans beat Pompey at Pharsalus, and he pardons his enemies, which Jiang calls "not very Roman" [Civ 15 · 40:40](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2440) [Civ 15 · 45:42](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2742).
- **General, politician and legislator in one.** His reforms (land reform, debt relief, the Julian calendar, wider citizenship) were meant to "make Rome great again". His secret was imagination, which let him be all three types of leader at once [Civ 15 · 25:28](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1528) [Civ 15 · 51:30](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3090) [Civ 15 · 57:35](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3455).
- **Why his friends killed him.** In Jiang's view he didn't want the crown. He saw himself as a better, more merciful Sulla who would fix Rome and retire, and he was killed because his myth outgrew Rome's old one, causing "cognitive dissonance" [Civ 15 · 04:44](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=284) [Civ 16 · 28:05](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1685). His evidence: Caesar went unguarded among senators, because violence inside the sacred Senate was unthinkable [Civ 16 · 24:17](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1457) [Civ 16 · 27:11](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1631).

**In his big picture:** Caesar is his template for the strongman: such figures "appear always at the end of a republic". Napoleon consciously copied him, Hitler followed the pattern, and Jiang asks whether Trump will too (see Chapters 9 and 11) [Civ 48 · 42:40](https://youtu.be/enEET_0j_Y8?t=2560) [Civ 48 · 55:48](https://youtu.be/enEET_0j_Y8?t=3348). Still, he ranks conquerors below poet-prophets, calling them "a culmination" of history rather than its creators [SH 21 · 1:13:35](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4415).

**In his words:** "Julius Caesar was so successful because he was a myth maker" [Civ 15 · 00:47](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=47)

**Reality check:** Excavations at Alise-Sainte-Reine have found Roman siege works at Alesia that match Caesar's account. The figures of a million Gauls killed and a million enslaved come from later writers such as Plutarch, and historians consider them inflated.

### Pompey

*Who:* Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus, "Pompey the Great" (106–48 BC), Rome's most celebrated general before Caesar. He was Caesar's ally in the First Triumvirate, then led the Senate's side in the civil war; defeated at Pharsalus, he was murdered in Egypt.

**What he teaches**
- **An ally of convenience.** Pompey, "considered the greatest general of Rome", was angry that the Senate refused land to his veterans. With Crassus he formed a secret pact with Caesar, the First Triumvirate, even though both men were optimates and Sulla's followers [Civ 15 · 33:40](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2020) [Civ 15 · 1:03:41](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3821). Caesar married his daughter to Pompey and later defeated his "mentor", a pattern Jiang also sees in Philip II and Genghis Khan [Civ 39 · 34:05](https://youtu.be/mPQzAPnjYkM?t=2045) [Civ 39 · 34:53](https://youtu.be/mPQzAPnjYkM?t=2093).
- **The strategy he wasn't allowed to use.** Holding the grain provinces, Pompey only had to blockade Italy and wait. But the optimates feared a long war would make him king, so they forced him into battle at Pharsalus, where Caesar's veterans beat him [Civ 15 · 42:33](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2553) [Civ 15 · 44:47](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2687) [Civ 15 · 47:30](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2850). In SH 21 he says they wanted both men destroyed [SH 21 · 34:57](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=2097).
- **Great soldier, poor politician.** Pompey is his example of a general without a politician's gifts [Civ 15 · 56:28](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3388).
- **Conqueror of Jerusalem.** Pompey's siege of Jerusalem in 63 BC killed and enslaved tens of thousands. Jiang counts it among the pressures that made Jews in Jesus's day fanatical about their religion (see Chapter 5) [Civ 24 · 28:52](https://youtu.be/_bXHBT5NTfc?t=1732).

**In his big picture:** Pompey shows how Rome's elite, fearing a king above all, sabotaged its own champion [Civ 15 · 44:47](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2687).

**In his words:** "Pompei was a great General but he's a terrible politician" [Civ 15 · 56:28](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3388)

### Cato the Younger

*Who:* Marcus Porcius Cato (95–46 BC), a Stoic senator and leader of the conservative optimates against Caesar. After Caesar's victory at Thapsus he killed himself at Utica rather than accept a pardon. His role as guardian of Purgatory in Dante is covered in Chapter 7.

**What he teaches**
- **Leader of the Caesar-haters.** Cato, Scipio and Cicero led the optimates who saw Caesar as the corruption of the Republic: a braggart and libertine chasing personal glory. Jiang calls their fixation "Caesar derangement syndrome" [Civ 15 · 30:58](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1858) [Civ 15 · 31:48](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1908).
- **Stopping him by the rules.** They made Caesar choose between his triumph and the consulship, then planned to keep him in Italy "building roads". Finally they moved to strip his command and put him on trial, which provoked the crossing of the Rubicon [Civ 15 · 31:48](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=1908) [Civ 15 · 33:40](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2020) [Civ 15 · 39:43](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2383).
- **Pushing Pompey to fight.** Distrusting Pompey, they forced a quick battle at Pharsalus. Caesar then pursued Cato and Scipio to Africa and beat them at Thapsus [Civ 15 · 47:30](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2850) [Civ 15 · 48:31](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=2911).
- **Death before submission.** Cato's suicide rather than submit to Caesar is what makes his place in Dante a paradox, which Jiang explores (see Chapter 7) [GB 10 · 35:09](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2109).

**In his big picture:** Cato stands for the Old Guard whose myth of Rome Caesar's new myth threatened [Civ 15 · 04:44](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=284).

**In his words:** "Cato chose to kill himself rather than submit to Julius Caesar." [GB 10 · 35:09](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2109)

### Cleopatra

*Who:* Cleopatra VII (69–30 BC), the last Ptolemaic ruler of Egypt. She was the lover of Julius Caesar and later the partner of Mark Antony, and the two killed themselves after their defeat by Octavian.

**What he teaches**
- **Caesar's mistress.** Rumour said Caesar would retire to live with her. Antony, trying to become Caesar, took her as his lover and divorced Octavian's sister for her, opening a personal rift with Octavian [Civ 16 · 34:43](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2083).
- **The will that doomed Antony.** Antony's will left Rome's eastern lands to his children by Cleopatra, who were foreigners, though only the Senate could grant such land. Octavian used it as a pretext for war and had the people behind him [Civ 16 · 35:40](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2140).
- **Actium.** At Actium she and Antony fled the battle. Octavian hunted them down in Alexandria, where both killed themselves [Civ 16 · 11:19](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=679).
- **The face of Greek corruption.** For Augustus, Antony was a good Roman who went to Egypt and was ruined by Cleopatra and Greek culture. That fear of Hellenism drove the Aeneid project [Civ 17 · 09:57](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=597) [Civ 17 · 11:01](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=661). Dante places her among the lustful, beside Dido (see Chapter 6) [GB 10 · 18:09](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=1089).

**In his big picture:** In his lectures Cleopatra is less a person than a symbol of the Greek East whose pull Augustus set out to resist.

**In his words:** "he became corrupted and seduced by Cleopatra and Greek culture" [Civ 17 · 11:01](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=661)

**Reality check:** Cleopatra wasn't waiting for Caesar to retire: she was living in Rome as his guest when he was killed in 44 BC.

### Mark Antony

*Who:* Marcus Antonius (83–30 BC), Caesar's lieutenant and consul in 44 BC. After the assassination he formed the Second Triumvirate with Octavian and Lepidus, then allied with Cleopatra and lost the final civil war at Actium.

**What he teaches**
- **Outplaying the assassins.** As consul he made peace with the conspirators, then quietly turned the people against them and read out Caesar's generous will, forcing them to flee Rome [Civ 16 · 00:00](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=0) [Civ 16 · 01:18](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=78).
- **Passed over.** He expected to be Caesar's heir. Caesar chose Octavian because he valued talent over loyalty, and Antony "was a notorious hot head", a drunk who had angered everyone while running Rome in Caesar's absence [Civ 16 · 41:13](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2473) [Civ 16 · 42:21](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2541).
- **Self-destruction by imitation.** To prove himself Caesar's true heir, he invaded Parthia and was beaten, sank into depression and drink, took up with Caesar's lover Cleopatra, and wrote the fatal will. Octavian barely had to fight [Civ 16 · 32:47](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1967) [Civ 16 · 33:52](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2032) [Civ 16 · 36:32](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2192).
- **The funeral speech.** In Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, Antony's speech collapses Brutus's neat antithesis (honourable Brutus against ambitious Caesar) through repeated chiasmus. For Jiang this is language as brain surgery, rewiring the crowd [Civ 51 · 34:50](https://youtu.be/Qms7trnKwqQ?t=2090) [Civ 51 · 37:03](https://youtu.be/Qms7trnKwqQ?t=2223).
- **Gone Greek.** Antony "saw himself as more Greek than Roman", which is how Augustus explained his betrayal [Civ 33 · 23:04](https://youtu.be/abs6z7VPEMc?t=1384).

**In his big picture:** Antony shows how Caesar's myth crushed those who tried to inherit it by imitation rather than belief.

**In his words:** "he's trying so hard to escape the shadow of Julius Caesar" [Civ 16 · 35:40](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2140)

**Reality check:** He says Antony "killed his own wife" [Civ 16 · 14:06](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=846). No ancient source reports this; his wife Fulvia died of illness in 40 BC.

### Marcus Junius Brutus

*Who:* Marcus Junius Brutus (85–42 BC), a senator who fought for Pompey, was pardoned and promoted by Caesar, and then led the conspiracy that killed him. He took his own life after the defeat at Philippi.

**What he teaches**
- **Caesar's favourite.** Brutus was the son of Caesar's favourite mistress. When he fought for Pompey, Caesar ordered him captured rather than killed, treated him as a son and fast-tracked his career. Romans even believed he was Caesar's biological son [Civ 16 · 20:34](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1234) [Civ 16 · 21:28](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1288) [Civ 16 · 22:20](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1340).
- **The vanity of a name.** Following Shakespeare, Jiang says Brutus was driven by his own sense of virtue, "basically vanity". Named after Lucius Brutus, he felt bound to kill any would-be king [Civ 16 · 21:28](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1288).
- **The antithesis.** Shakespeare's Brutus argues by antithesis, "not that I love Caesar less but I love Rome more": honour and ambition can't coexist, so ambitious Caesar had to die [Civ 51 · 34:09](https://youtu.be/Qms7trnKwqQ?t=2049) [Civ 51 · 36:22](https://youtu.be/Qms7trnKwqQ?t=2182).
- **Paralysed by the deed.** After the murder the conspirators realized Caesar can't have wanted a crown, or he would have kept bodyguards. When Cassius urged him to rescue Decimus Brutus, he did nothing, because marching on Rome would make him the ambitious one [Civ 16 · 29:00](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1740) [Civ 16 · 30:46](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1846). He died after the Battle of Philippi [Civ 16 · 10:19](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=619).
- **In Satan's mouth.** Dante puts him and Cassius beside Judas in Lucifer's jaws. Jiang calls this a paradox and suggests that Virgil, Augustus's poet, is the one who put them there (see Chapter 6) [GB 10 · 32:35](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=1955) [GB 10 · 33:53](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=2033).

**In his big picture:** Brutus is his case study of a man trapped by an old myth: killing Caesar in its name destroyed the Republic he meant to save.

**In his words:** "he could not act he killed Julius Caesar because Julius Caesar was too ambitious" [Civ 16 · 30:46](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1846)

**Reality check:** The rumour that Caesar fathered Brutus is ancient gossip reported by Plutarch. Most historians doubt it, since Caesar was about fifteen when Brutus was born.

### Gaius Cassius Longinus

*Who:* Gaius Cassius Longinus (c. 86–42 BC), an officer on Pompey's side whom Caesar pardoned and employed. He co-led the assassination and killed himself at Philippi.

**What he teaches**
- **A pardoned enemy.** Cassius fought for Pompey, but Caesar thought him such a fine general that he pardoned him and made him one of his own [Civ 15 · 58:42](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=3522).
- **Mercy as insult.** "in the Roman World Mercy is not considered a good", Jiang says, so Caesar's clemency may have read to Cassius as contempt, as though he were no threat. His motive may have been vengeance [Civ 16 · 19:30](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1170) [Civ 16 · 20:34](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1234).
- **The practical conspirator.** After the murder he urged Brutus to help Decimus Brutus, since the conspirators would be next. Brutus wouldn't move [Civ 16 · 30:46](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1846).
- **Punished beside Judas.** With Brutus, he is chewed by Lucifer in Dante's Hell. Jiang finds this illogical, since Caesar himself is only in Limbo (see Chapter 6) [GB 10 · 32:35](https://youtu.be/wGpdMYa2bME?t=1955).

**In his big picture:** Cassius shows the paradox of Caesar's clemency: in a culture of honour, forgiveness can wound.

**In his words:** "in the Roman World Mercy is not considered a good" [Civ 16 · 19:30](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1170)

### Augustus (Octavian)

*Who:* Gaius Octavius (63 BC–AD 14), Caesar's great-nephew and adopted heir, whom historians call Octavian. He defeated the assassins and then Antony and Cleopatra (Actium, 31 BC), received the title Augustus in 27 BC, and ruled as Rome's first emperor until his death.

**What he teaches**
- **The least likely winner.** At eighteen, with no army or allies, he faced a "Game of Thrones" against Antony, Lepidus, Cicero and the assassins, yet destroyed them one by one [Civ 16 · 05:28](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=328) [Civ 16 · 08:12](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=492). He even borrowed money to pay Caesar's bequest to every citizen [Civ 16 · 04:30](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=270).
- **Carried by guilt.** Jiang rejects five explanations for his rise (luck, brilliance, ruthlessness, the legions, Agrippa). His own: Caesar's death made Caesar's myth real, and the people's guilt at having doubted Caesar became love for his son, just as Canadians transferred their love of Pierre Trudeau to Justin [Civ 16 · 15:06](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=906) [Civ 16 · 16:15](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=975) [Civ 16 · 17:17](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1037). Believing himself Caesar's heir, Octavian acted while others froze [Civ 16 · 37:29](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2249).
- **Power built quietly.** He made Egypt his private estate, used its wealth to pay a professional army and a Praetorian Guard loyal only to him, and settled veterans on land in Gaul. Romans allowed it "because they believed him too much" [Civ 16 · 39:13](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2353) [Civ 16 · 40:02](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2402) [Civ 17 · 05:42](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=342). He called himself *princeps*, "first citizen", and claimed to be saving the Republic [Civ 32 · 35:31](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2131).
- **The culture war.** He faced three problems: legitimacy, an ideal of liberty that would inspire more Brutuses, and Greek culture seducing Romans. The Aeneid answered all three [Civ 17 · 06:45](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=405) [Civ 17 · 08:56](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=536) [Civ 17 · 13:21](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=801). By 2025 Jiang adds that Augustus had Livy rewrite Polybius's history "from a Roman lens" [SH 21 · 37:41](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=2261).
- **The "Jesus of Rome".** The Aeneid makes Augustus the endpoint of history, whose Pax Romana would bring eternal peace, an idea of eternity Jiang says Rome took from Egypt [Civ 17 · 54:11](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3251) [Civ 17 · 58:34](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3514) [Civ 21 · 46:31](https://youtu.be/RkpGrRUgjnk?t=2791). The Church later saw Jesus's birth under Augustus as providential (see Chapter 5) [Dante 7 · 1:24:51](https://youtu.be/P82v6UAc9ss?t=5091).
- **A succession that failed.** He planned an adoptive system, with Tiberius as a bridge to the brilliant Germanicus, and it broke down at once [Civ 16 · 47:40](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2860).
- **A template for Trump.** At an August 2026 meet-up, Jiang predicted that if Trump wins a third term he will copy Augustus's three moves. First, a privately paid army, as Egypt paid for Augustus's; ICE, he says, shows it beginning. Second, co-opting factions by handing them provinces, which for Trump means colonizing the Western Hemisphere for his allies. Third, a religious revival like the one Augustus promoted with the Aeneid (see Chapter 11) [Meet-Up 4 · 12:27](https://youtu.be/5gyyAzfmWk4?t=747) [Meet-Up 4 · 13:34](https://youtu.be/5gyyAzfmWk4?t=814) [Meet-Up 4 · 16:54](https://youtu.be/5gyyAzfmWk4?t=1014).

**In his big picture:** Augustus turns Rome from a republic of liberty into an empire of piety. In Jiang's story he is the bridge from Rome to the Catholic Church [GB 8 · 01:32](https://youtu.be/YXTRlvFIRt8?t=92).

**In his words:** "the death of Caesar allow Octavian to become emperor" [Civ 16 · 15:06](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=906)

### Tiberius

*Who:* Tiberius Claudius Nero (42 BC–AD 37), Augustus's stepson and adopted heir, and Rome's second emperor (AD 14–37).

**What he teaches**
- **The caretaker who wouldn't step aside.** Augustus meant the older, competent but uncharismatic Tiberius to rule only until Germanicus came of age. Tiberius wanted to choose his own successor [Civ 16 · 48:36](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2916).
- **Purges.** He killed Germanicus and most of his family, then made Germanicus's youngest son, Caligula, "a notoriously bad Emperor", his heir [Civ 16 · 49:26](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2966). In Civ 32 the killing runs through the Praetorian prefect Sejanus, whom Tiberius then has killed, "a very common pattern in imperial history" [Civ 32 · 40:00](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2400).
- **Consider the source.** Our picture of Tiberius, Caligula and Nero comes from senators such as Tacitus, who hated the emperors and made them look bad [Civ 32 · 37:42](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2262) [Civ 32 · 38:46](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2326).

**In his big picture:** Tiberius marks the failure of the Augustan settlement. After him, Jiang says, the empire lasted three more centuries only through its size and inertia [Civ 16 · 49:26](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2966).

**In his words:** "you could make the argument that tiberious marked the death of the Roman Empire" [Civ 16 · 49:26](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=2966)

**Reality check:** Germanicus died in Syria in AD 19. Poisoning was suspected and ancient writers hinted at Tiberius, but nothing was proven.

## Key ideas from these lectures

- **Piety, liberty, res publica**: Rome's three defining values: duty to gods, city and father; liberty as obedience to law, custom and institutions, above all meaning no kings; and public virtue, a competition to serve Rome. He sets them against the Greek trio of freedom of speech, flourishing (*eudaimonia*) and excellence (*arete*) [Civ 14 · 28:42](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1722) [Civ 32 · 13:18](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=798) [Civ 32 · 17:25](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=1045).
- **Cohesion, discipline, devotion**: his replacement for manpower, technology and money as the real measure of military strength, and the reason "Nations win Wars" [Civ 14 · 23:01](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=1381).
- **Republic versus democracy**: a democracy decides by debate and vote, so it guards citizenship jealously. A republic decides by tradition, as in "every Thursday we go out to get dumplings", so it can absorb newcomers who accept its customs and keep refilling its armies [Civ 32 · 20:28](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=1228) [Civ 32 · 21:23](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=1283). His lesson for today is that cheap citizenship dissolves a nation, as he argues is happening in Canada [Civ 32 · 1:00:34](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=3634).
- **History as mythology**: the Greeks separated myth from history, but the Romans raised their history into a religion taught to every new citizen. "what matters is what they believe as opposed to what really happened" [Civ 14 · 33:32](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=2012) [Civ 14 · 1:02:05](https://youtu.be/TdcE7XGDayo?t=3725).
- **The imperial republic**: once Rome ruled the Mediterranean it was an empire that couldn't admit it, and the contradiction bred corruption, inequality and endless civil war. Americans, he argues, have the same "cognitive dissonance" about their own empire [Civ 15 · 10:37](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=637) [Civ 32 · 32:42](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=1962).
- **Optimates and populares**: Rome's civil wars were fights inside the elite, between the established "best men" and ambitious lesser nobles who courted the people. He links this to Peter Turchin's "elite overproduction": "It's never between rich and poor" [Civ 15 · 15:18](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=918) [SH 21 · 1:24:46](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=5086).
- **Mythmaker**: a leader who sees himself as a man of destiny and builds a new reality through words and deeds, as Caesar did. The price is the cognitive dissonance that can get him killed [Civ 15 · 00:47](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=47) [Civ 15 · 04:44](https://youtu.be/F8qQgsEFGgc?t=284).
- **Sacred taboos**: the *pomerium* (the sacred city boundary where no weapons were allowed), the holiness of the Senate and the horror of kings. These taboos made Caesar feel safe among his killers [Civ 16 · 24:17](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1457) [Civ 16 · 25:18](https://youtu.be/Dc54IquR7ik?t=1518).
- **Princeps and "the glory of obedience"**: Augustus called himself "first citizen", never emperor [Civ 32 · 35:31](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2131). A generation later, in Tacitus's Annals, the senator Marcus Terentius defends himself by saying subjects have only "the glory of obedience". For Jiang this shows how empire changed the Roman character, and why Tacitus recorded it [Civ 32 · 42:53](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2573) [Civ 32 · 46:39](https://youtu.be/_Io7-_RS8uE?t=2799).
- **Pax Romana and the end of history**: the Aeneid's claim that all history led to Augustus, whose peace would last forever. He contrasts this Roman "eternity", which he says came from Egypt, with the Greek "infinity" of human creativity [Civ 17 · 54:11](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3251) [Civ 17 · 57:35](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=3455) [Civ 31 · 17:59](https://youtu.be/HIoYQBbBllk?t=1079).
- **The anti-Homer**: his name for the Aeneid, which inverts Homer so that "love is hell" and "Heaven is piety". The real Trojan horse, in this reading, is Greek culture [GB 7 · 28:37](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=1717) [GB 7 · 28:58](https://youtu.be/EBWTRvjZ1dw?t=1738) [Civ 17 · 43:01](https://youtu.be/myTurqU7NxU?t=2581).
- **Roman anti-civilization**: his 2025 label for Rome as an empire "based on hatred": gladiators instead of tragedy, drunken feasts instead of symposia. He calls those feasts, which re-enacted Rome's founding myths, the beginning of secret societies, and presents this world as the one Jesus answered (see Glossary and Chapters 5 and 10) [SH 21 · 1:08:46](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4126) [SH 21 · 1:11:06](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4266) [SH 21 · 1:11:52](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=4312). He repeats the story that Romans vomited between courses, which is largely a modern myth.
- **Trauma is a drug**: the idea that Rome deliberately used guilt and trauma, as in Lucius Brutus's execution of his sons, to generate "demonic energy" against its enemies [SH 21 · 53:15](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3195) [SH 21 · 55:26](https://youtu.be/Y-gXVzwbFMU?t=3326).
- **The war machine turns inward**: Rome and America are aggressive societies that fight themselves once they run out of foreign enemies.
